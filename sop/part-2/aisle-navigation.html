Aisle Navigation

    Part II — Aisle Navigation

    Two kinds of sign tell you where things are. Aisle markers hang over the aisle. Outriggers stick out from the gondola. Between them, they'll take you from the front of the store to the eight feet you've been assigned.

    Learning to read them is the fastest way to stop needing directions.

    Aisle markers

    The aisle marker hangs over the aisle and lists the commodities in it. Two styles of sign, read the same way.

    The order is a size ranking

    The commodities listed are the ones with the most footage in that aisle, arranged largest to smallest.

    So the first thing on the sign is the biggest commodity down there — not the most important one, and not the one nearest the front. Read it that way and you can tell how an aisle is weighted before you walk into it.

    Commodities too small to earn a place aren't listed at all. An aisle contains more than its sign says.

    Conventional and natural

    Two inserts that look like the same commodity — candy and natural candy — are conventional and Natural Foods listed separately.

    They sit side by side, or on a vertical sign, conventional first with natural directly below it.

    Conventional gets listed first and natural is added where there's room, so a natural insert on the sign means that aisle carries enough natural product in that commodity to be worth calling out.

    Blanks

    A blank is a filler panel in a slot with no verbiage on it.

    It means the sign is complete and that slot was spare. It doesn't mean anything is missing.

    Outriggers

    Outriggers mount to the gondola and mark individual sets within the aisle. The aisle marker gets you to the right aisle; the outrigger gets you to the right eight feet of it. When your Kompass Notes name a commodity, the outrigger is what you're scanning for as you walk.

    Aisle sequence

    Sequence is the order sets are laid out along the aisle. Your planogram sits in that order.

    This is the useful one when something looks wrong. A set that appears to be in the wrong place is usually a sequence question, not a planogram error — the aisle is laid out to a plan, and the markers and outriggers were built from that same plan.

    The store-layout term for the same idea is adjacency: how sets sit next to each other along the run.

Conditioning by Department

    Part II — Conditioning and Stocking by Department

    Conditioning is bringing a shelf back to how it's meant to look — product pulled forward, faced, straightened, gaps closed. It's what makes a set look stocked when it's half sold.

    Two things to know before the department rules:

    You condition what you worked, and the scope follows the project.

    Project Condition
    Full resetThe entire set you reset
    NII, partial, maintenanceThe area you worked, and no further

    This isn't about doing less. Work is time-allocated, and an NII may be allocated fifteen minutes. Conditioning a forty-foot aisle is not inside fifteen minutes, and pretending otherwise means either the job runs long or the actual work gets rushed to make room for it.

    It's the same discipline as Section 8.6: you touch what you were sent to touch. Work outside your area isn't tracked, isn't signed off, and comes out of the next job.

    Every department does this differently, and the differences aren't arbitrary. Grocery is pulled forward. Frozen is stocked back to front. Dairy is built like a wall. Each one follows from how the product sells and how the case is built, so the rule that feels wrong in one department is the right rule in another.

    Grocery

    Unstacked product — where the strip and planogram show no stacking:

    • Pull three items forward for each facing
    • Everything else stays pushed to the back of the shelf

    Stacked product — where the strip and planogram call for it:

    • First layer: front to back
    • Every layer above that: back to front

    That alternation is what stops the stack leaning forward. Build every layer front-to-back and the top of the stack walks toward the shelf edge.

    Caps go on top. A cap is a unit laid flat over standing product — the C in the C/H/W notation on the strip. See Section 7.7.

    CSD 12-packs: handles face right. Every one, so the row reads as a block rather than a jumble.

    Chips and salty snacks are the exception to all of the above. They get pushed to the back for the vendor to fill. Don't pull them forward. This is DSD product and the vendor works it on their own schedule.

    Drug / GM

    Most of this department is in pushers, and the pusher does the conditioning for you.

    • Product not in a pusher gets pulled forward
    • Never stock behind a pusher. The pusher is the back of the space — product behind it can't come forward and won't be found
    • Only lay product on top of pusher product if the planogram says to. Otherwise the paddle jams
    • Every peg hook needs an inventory control clip, with product merchandised to the front of the peg. The ICC is what holds it there — a peg without one drifts back within a day and reads as empty from down the aisle

    See Part II, Special Fixtures for the pusher and peg hardware itself.

    Dairy

    Build it like a wall. Left to right, and upward where the product stacks.

    • Fill the front of the shelf completely before you start filling toward the back
    • All empty space ends up at the back of the shelf, never at the front

    That's the opposite of the grocery habit of pulling forward, and it's deliberate: dairy sells fast and gets refilled constantly, so the case is worked from the front and the gap belongs where the customer doesn't see it.

    Clean before you reload. Back walls of the cases, the wire grates, the area under the grates — the well — and the shelves. The well is the part everyone skips and it's the part that gets checked.

    Milk racks carry two sets of strips: one customer-facing, one inside the cooler. Set one and you've done half the job. Anyone loading from the back of the rack is reading the inside strip.

    Frozen

    Everything in frozen is stocked back to front. The reverse of grocery.

    On the bottom shelf — the well — lay product down wherever possible.

    When a shelf holds more than one layer:

    • The lays go down first, flat on the shelf
    • The standing product goes on top of the lays
    • Not the other way round. Product laid across the top of standing items is unstable, hides what's underneath, and falls out when a customer takes one

    Every product gets as many lays as will fit, plus one standing item. The standing item is the one the customer reads.

    Top shelf: two standing items pulled forward.

    Pizza is the exception — all pizzas on the bottom shelf stand. They're wide and flat and a stack of laid pizzas can't be got at.

    Items marked SRP on the strip stay in their display boxes. Don't unpack them.

    Twenty minutes out of the case, maximum. Then it goes into the walk-in freezer. Watch which one you put it in — frozen product in a cooler is a write-off, and so is the reverse.

Special Fixtures

    Part II — Special Fixtures

    A shelf is a shelf. Everything else on the front edge, the back wall, or hanging off the gondola is a fixture with a job — holding product forward as stock runs down, keeping items that won't stack from falling over, making things reachable, or turning a flat gondola into something that displays a spatula.

    This section is for looking things up. You don't need to read it front to back. When you get to a set and find hardware you don't recognize, find it here.

    One thing to get straight before anything else: "rail" means two different parts. A pusher rail is the plastic track along the shelf front that pushers and end pieces lock into. An ESL rail is the clip-in channel that holds a digital shelf label. They sit in the same place and do unrelated jobs. Section 8, Step 4 covers the ESL rail.

    Pushers

    A pusher is a spring-loaded fixture that keeps product pushed to the front of the shelf as stock sells down. It's the reason a DGM shelf still looks full with four items left on it.

    The three components

    Part What it is
    RailThe plastic track fitted along the front of the shelf. Everything else locks into it
    Pusher / dividerThe spring-loaded paddle on a track. Its length matches or is shorter than the shelf depth. The paddle rotates and sets to different positions to take different product widths and heights. Comes in single and double
    End piecesLeft and right side pieces. Ends go only at the beginning and end of the set — not between bays if the set continues across several

    That last rule catches people. If a category runs across three bays, you get one left end at the very start and one right end at the very finish. Putting ends at every bay seam breaks the run into three sets that look like they were done by three different people.

    Setting a pusher shelf

    • Lay out the pusher positions before you fill anything. Space them evenly across the shelf first, then load. Filling as you go leaves you with the last pusher crammed against the end piece
    • Don't stock product behind a pusher. The pusher is the back of the space. Product behind it can't move forward and won't be found
    • Product not in a pusher gets pulled forward — that's the general DGM rule
    • Only lay product on top of pusher product if the planogram says so. Otherwise the paddle jams

    Coffee pushers

    Different shape, same three components, installed the same way. Don't be thrown by the profile.

    Grid pushers — smoked meat, frozen meat and seafood, bagged salad

    These work differently and the planogram will mislead you if you don't know it.

    • A metal grid mounts to the back wall of the freezer or refrigerated case
    • Two-part pushers hang on that grid
    • The planogram may show these sections as ordinary shelves. They aren't. Each facing sits in its own separate pusher
    • Lay out the pusher spacing across the grid before filling, same as any pusher shelf

    Air baffles. If a smoked meat or produce set has no doors, plastic air baffles go between each row of pushers. These are not optional trim — they're what makes the cold air circulate through the case. Leave them out and the product at the front of the case sits warmer than it should.

    In Produce: pre-packaged and bagged salad is always in pushers. Carrots and mushrooms are sometimes in pushers — it varies store to store, so look before you assume.

    DSL note

    Bagged salad pushers have their own digital label holder — the Salad Pusher System Adapter ESL Holder. If you're in a DSL store setting salad, that's the part you're looking for. The parts reference has the number.

    Pegboards and bump-outs

    Peg hooks

    • 11-inch peg hooks are standard on peg sets
    • His Shave and Her Shave use 8-inch pegs — unless the Relay Manager instructs otherwise
    • Checklanes use 6-inch pegs. See Part II, Front End
    • Every peg hook needs an inventory control clip (ICC) with the product merchandised to the front of the peg. The ICC is what holds it there. A peg without one drifts back within a day and reads as empty from down the aisle

    Hook types you'll meet: fast-back hooks (two-prong, sometimes two-part for easy removal), J hooks (mounted on a shelf rather than a board, usually impulse product), and sky hook pegs.

    Frogs

    The frog is the plastic clip that seats into the pegboard and holds the peg hook. Set one on a table and you'll see where the name comes from — the two legs and the body read as a frog sitting there.

    A frog occupies two holes, and that raises an obvious question: which hole is the position?

    The left leg goes into the hole specified.

    That's the whole convention, and it's worth committing to memory, because a frog seated one hole off puts the product one inch off — and every peg you hang after it inherits the error.

    Combined with the one-inch rule: the specified hole takes the left leg, and the frog spans that hole and the one immediately to its right.

    The one-inch rule

    Pegboard holes are exactly one inch apart, center to center — both across and down.

    Learn this one. It converts every peg instruction in this document from counting to measuring, and a tape measure finds a position faster and more reliably than your eyes count holes.

    • Three holes over is three inches over
    • A 3 × 3 hardware grid is a three-inch grid
    • A peg position from the schematic can be measured to instead of counted to

    Bump-outs — what they are

    Reference, not a task. You won't build one of these. Construction does. But you will move them, audit them, and work around them, so you need to recognize what you're looking at and be able to tell whether one is right.

    A bump-out is a pegboard extended away from the gondola, so pegged product sits further forward and is easier for a customer to reach. All peg sets get bumped out. Trial size isn't on pegs — it has its own bins — and those get bumped out too.

    Three components: bars, brackets, and the pegboard itself.

    Component What it does
    PegboardThe perforated board, cut to size for the bay
    BracketsNine of them, mounted to the back of the board on a 3 × 3 grid. The corner brackets sit three holes in from each edge — three inches, by the one-inch rule
    Extender barsThree bars hung on the gondola the same way a shelf hangs. The board hangs on these

    What "right" looks like: the board sits square and doesn't rock, and all nine brackets are seated on the bars. A board that moves when you push it has a bracket that isn't seated, and that's worth reporting rather than living with — a loaded pegboard that comes off the bars takes the product with it.

    Magna-Bars

    This one you do work on. Moving, resetting, and hanging Magna-Bars is regular work, so know the system rather than just recognizing it.

    Not the same as a bump-out, though they're close cousins. A bump-out hangs a pegboard on bars. A Magna-Bar is the bar, with hooks going straight into it and no board at all. Similar hardware family, different system — don't order one expecting the other.

    A Magna-Bar is a peg merchandising system that mounts to the gondola uprights directly. Peg hooks hang off the bar itself, so there's no pegboard involved — it's an alternative to a peg wall, not a component of one. Bars come in horizontal and vertical, and the system takes ID channels that carry the shelf strip.

    Components, per the manufacturer's kit of four bars:

    Part Count per 4 bars
    Bars4
    Brackets8 — one at each end of each bar
    Uprights8
    Flat plates8
    Screws16

    FRONT AND UP

    The bar is stamped FRONT AND UP. That stamp is not a label — it's the mechanism.

    The end of the peg hook that goes into the bar is D-shaped, and so are the slots along the bar. The flat of the D has to meet the flat of the slot. Get that right and the hook drops in and locks. Get it wrong and the shapes don't marry up — the hook either won't go in, or it sits in the slot without ever locking, which is worse, because it looks fine until the product on it takes it off the wall.

    So:

    Mount the bar with FRONT AND UP reading correctly, and the hooks lock. Mount it inverted or reversed, and they don't.

    When you pull a bar to move it, check the stamp on the way back in. Don't trust the orientation you found it in — if it was wrong before, putting it back the same way keeps it wrong, and now it's wrong on your set.

    If a hook won't seat, or seats but slides back out, check the bar's orientation before you do anything else. That's the cause far more often than a damaged hook.

    Load limit: 150 lbs per bar. A sagging bar is a report, not something to shim.

    What "right" looks like: FRONT AND UP reading correctly, brackets seated in the upright slots at both ends, all screws tight, bar level, and hooks that lock in rather than rest in.

    Dividers and clearview

    Both keep product that doesn't stack well from sliding, leaning, or migrating into the next facing. Both attach to the shelf with plastic "Christmas tree" clips.

    Fixture What it does
    DividerA plastic insert that separates facings — a wall between one product and the next
    ClearviewA clear plastic strip mounted along the shelf front that stops items sliding off

    Fencing is the same idea under an older name from the acquired companies — an acrylic or metal rail along the shelf front.

    Slides

    A slide does what a divider does, except the product sits inside it. That makes stocking and conditioning faster — you pull the whole row forward instead of straightening each item.

    Slide Where
    Spice slideNumbered by size. Spices, canned cat food, and dry desserts (gelatin and pudding). Check the number — they aren't interchangeable
    Yogurt slideHas a built-in handle to pull the row forward
    Creamer slideRefrigerated coffee creamer in dairy, mounted on the milk racks

    In-line single-serve cases

    Single-serve beverage cases use roller shelves — the shelf floor is a set of rollers, so when a customer takes a can the row behind it flows forward on its own.

    Two things have to be right or it doesn't work:

    • Every facing or row needs a divider. Without one, rows merge and the flow jams
    • Clearview must be snapped securely into the front of each roller shelf. It's the only thing stopping the front can rolling onto the floor

    Kitchen Place and the in-line kitchen grid

    Household kitchen accessories don't sit on ordinary shelves. They're merchandised on grids, and which style you find depends on the store format.

    Format Where
    Kitchen PlaceMarketplace, Expanded, and Upscale stores. Free-standing gridwall with display tables in front
    In-line kitchen gridFood and Value stores. Grid panels set into the run itself

    Both hang product from the grid rather than standing it on a shelf, so peg and hook rules apply rather than facing and stack rules.

    One storage exception worth knowing: there is no top stock in Kitchen Place stores. See Part II, Storage.

Storage

    Part II — Storage

    Excess product is anything that won't fit on the shelf.

    Before you decide where it goes, understand why there's excess at all. Inventory and allocations are calculated from the shelf's depth capacity — how much the set is designed to hold. When a shelf is stocked exactly as the planogram says, the ordering system is working from a true number. When it isn't, the store orders against a shelf that doesn't exist.

    So the first rule of storage is a setting rule: stock the shelf exactly as the planogram indicates, then deal with what's left over. Don't cram an extra layer on to avoid a trip to the back.

    Excess goes to one of three places: top stock, a U-boat, or backstock storage.

    Top stock

    The shelf above the set. Some older paperwork calls it a riser.

    Why it exists: product on top stock is above the set it belongs to, in the aisle, where anyone can reach it. That keeps the back room emptier and makes replenishment something a store associate does in thirty seconds instead of a trip to the back and a hunt through a pallet.

    Setting top stock

    • Pull items forward, labels facing forward
    • Leave the left-hand item turned so its UPC faces out. Someone scanning to replenish shouldn't have to lift and rotate a case to find the barcode
    • No more than three high
    • Product must sit above the set it belongs to. Don't top stock pasta above mustard — top stock is a location system as well as a storage one, and product above the wrong set is lost
    • Tray packs and SRP product can go on the top shelf directly, labels forward, left item's UPC turned out
    • Unopened cases don't get top stocked — tray packs and SRP excepted
    • Large or heavy items don't go up top at all. Charcoal, dog food, bulk liquids. Those go on a Fast or Active backstock cart instead. A case you have to lift over your head is a case you shouldn't be lifting over your head
    • Organize the top shelf as you go. It's maintained daily, not rebuilt weekly

    Where top stock is not allowed

    Exception
    Kitchen Place storesNo top stock at all. See Part II, Special Fixtures
    Infant formulaNever top stocked
    From a displayNo more than two cases on the top shelf above the home location

    U-boats

    A U-boat is the green six-wheeled cart — narrow, and U-shaped in profile, which is where the name comes from. Fred Meyer uses them for vendor stock and backstock.

    • Get them from the receiver in the back room
    • Walked, never ridden or coasted. Six wheels and a long narrow frame means it goes where it's pointed and keeps going
    • Don't leave a loaded U-boat in an aisle. Same rule as carts — if nobody's actively working off it, it's out of the aisle
    • Return them to the receiver at the end of the shift

    Backstock storage

    What doesn't go up top and isn't on a cart goes into containers.

    What to put it in

    Banana boxes, first and almost always.

    They're the container of choice for this work and they're what you'll use nearly every shift. Sturdy, stackable, a sensible size for a person to carry loaded, and with a lid that lets them stack without crushing what's underneath. Get them from produce or from the receiver.

    Blue Peyton totes second, when banana boxes aren't available.

    Beyond those two, ask your lead. Other containers exist in the store — bread trays, meat crates, egg crates, produce crates, milk crates — and older reference material lists them as fallbacks, but they aren't what this division uses and you shouldn't reach for one on your own initiative.

    Stacking

    Don't stack more than 6 high, and use judgment below that — a stack you can't see over, or can't get a hand under to move, is too tall regardless of the count.

    Two rules that aren't about tidiness

    • Never mix food and chemicals on the same pallet. That's a food safety issue, and it's the kind of thing that gets noticed in an audit long after everyone's forgotten who stacked it
    • Never store liquids on their sides. Under the pressure of a stack, they leak — and a leak inside a stack isn't found until it's soaked through several layers

    Handing it off

    A boxed-up box isn't finished. It has to end up somewhere someone will act on.

    Ask the receiver first. If they can take it now, or soon, leave it with them. That's the clean outcome — it's their process from that point and it doesn't sit anywhere.

    If they won't have time soon, palletize it. A stack of boxes on the floor of the back room is in someone's way and will be moved by someone who doesn't know what's in it. On a pallet it's stable, it's movable, and it stays together.

    Either way, every box is clearly labeled. This is the part that gets skipped at the end of a shift and it's the part that matters most. An unlabeled box is a guess — for the receiver, for the next team in, for whoever eventually opens it. A pallet of unlabeled boxes is a pallet nobody unpacks, and it sits in the back room until someone gives up and works through it one box at a time.

    Label it with the category:

    Backstock · Discontinued · Not In Set · Damaged · Expired

    One of those five, on every box. That's what the receiver sorts by, and it's the difference between a box that gets processed and a box that gets opened, puzzled over, and set down again.

    Label as you fill, not at the end. You know what's in the box while you're putting it in there.

    Sorting what comes off the shelf

    At the start of the shift your lead designates and signs an area for each of these. Ask where they are before you need them.

    The five box labels are the five categories. Sort into them as you pull, and the labeling takes care of itself.

    Label What goes in it
    BackstockProduct that won't fit on the shelf. Top stock or a U-boat first; what's left gets boxed
    DiscontinuedDIs per the Kompass Notes, carrying closeout tags
    Not In SetProduct that isn't on this planogram. Marked down for clearance or returned to the DSD vendor
    DamagedTorn boxes, cut bags, smashed cans, leaking cartons. Not sellable
    ExpiredPast its sell-by or expiration date

    Trash — cardboard, plastic, waste — isn't boxed or labeled. It's disposed of properly as you go.

    Everything from one set stays together. If the pickle set comes off, all of it goes in the same place — carts, totes, milk crates, bread trays, whatever you're using — until it goes back to its permanent home. Splitting a pulled set across three locations turns a twenty-minute job into an hour.

    Take it to its area within a reasonable time. Not at the end of the shift. Carts full of product are not left in aisles, and a cart that isn't being actively worked comes out of the aisle — see Section 8.1 for the one-cart-per-person rule.

    Refrigerated and frozen product gets 20 minutes out of the case, then it goes into the walk-in cooler or freezer. Check which one you're putting it in — refrigerated product in a freezer is a write-off, and so is the reverse.

    Bakery runs tighter — 15 minutes. Frosting, cream, and glazes go before anything else does. See Part II, Bakery.

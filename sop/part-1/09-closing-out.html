Section 9 — Closing Out

    Section 9 — Closing Out

    Section 8 tells you how to close out a set. This section is about closing out the shift — the last thirty minutes, when the work is done and it would be easy to leave a store that looks like a reset happened in it.

    That last part matters more than it sounds. The store team sees the aisle after you're gone, and they form their whole opinion of us from it. A perfect set in a wrecked aisle reads as a bad visit.

    9.1 — Walk the set

    Every set gets walked before it's called done. This one has no exceptions.

    Who you walk it with depends on your role:

    You walk with
    RepYour lead
    LeadThe store

    Walk it before you clean up, while everything is still out and fixable. This is the cheapest correction in the whole process — a facing count spotted in thirty seconds costs two minutes to fix with the cart still beside you, and a return trip if it's found after you've gone.

    There's a reason it's someone else's eyes. By the time you finish a set you can't see it fresh any more. You've looked at it for an hour, you know what you intended, and you'll read the shelf as the thing you meant to build. Somebody walking up cold sees what's actually there.

    Reps: have the planogram in your hand when your lead walks up. Your lead will check the things Section 8's verify step lists.

    Leads: walking the completed work with the store is how the visit closes. It's also where anything that couldn't be finished gets said out loud rather than discovered later.

    If you highlighted anything on the planogram — a position you couldn't tag, a facing you had to reduce — hand it over on the walk and say what you did. Section 8, Step 8.

    9.2 — Clear the aisle

    Nothing you brought in stays in the aisle. Not a cart, not a tote, not a bread tray, not a milk crate, not an empty box you meant to come back for.

    Work through this:

    • Carts, totes, trays, and crates out of the aisle entirely
    • Product from the pull categories delivered where it goes — damaged and out-of-date to the receiver, backstock to the store associate or into labeled boxes, not-in-set product routed per your lead
    • Trash, cardboard, and plastic disposed of properly. Cardboard goes to the baler area — you do not operate the baler unless you're 18 or over and someone has shown you that specific machine
    • Spills swept or cleaned, including the ones that were there when you arrived
    • What you worked, conditioned to standard. On a full reset that's the entire set. On an NII or a partial, it's the area you worked — see Part II, Conditioning by Department

    On scope: a partial may be allocated fifteen minutes, and conditioning a whole aisle isn't inside fifteen minutes. Condition what you were sent to do, and do it properly, rather than spreading thin across work that isn't yours.

    9.3 — Return what isn't yours

    • Borrowed equipment back to the receiver — hand trucks, dollies, U-boats, step ladders
    • Shopping carts emptied and returned to the corral. Every one. The cart exception in Section 8.1 holds because we return them; it stops holding the first time a team leaves six carts in an aisle overnight
    • Your personal items collected from wherever your lead designated at the start of the shift
    • Nothing of the store's goes home with you — not damaged product, not discontinued product, not an empty display piece. Section 8, Step 1

    9.4 — Sign-off

    You don't touch the sign-off paperwork. That's your lead's job everywhere, without exception. You should still understand how it works, because the quality of what you hand your lead determines how quickly it goes.

    Photographing your completed work is different. Some districts have the rep photograph their own sets; others have the lead do it. Your lead will tell you which applies to you — ask on your first shift rather than assuming. If it's you, 9.5 is the standard.

    How the work gets signed out:

    • Fred Meyer signs off on completed Kompass work
    • Two methods are in use right now — fax and photo. Which one applies depends on the store and the project. Your lead will know; if you're the lead and you don't, ask me
    • The store's sign-off is usually coordinated by the Kompass Champion, though that role is rarely staffed anymore. In practice most of the coordination falls to your lead

    What we do not do:

    CAO and Kompass Completion are Fred Meyer systems. We have no logins for them, and we are not permitted to sign work out in them. If anyone in a store asks you to complete work in either system, the answer is no, and you tell your lead. This isn't a preference — it's the boundary between our work and theirs, and crossing it creates a record nobody can defend.

    Ratings and the store visit summary still exist in the process. They aren't heavily used at the moment, and where they are, they're the manager's step rather than something you fill in.

    9.5 — Photographing completed work

    If photographing your sets is part of your role, you'll be set up with a Store Intelligence login and you submit the photos yourself, directly in the app. Ask me if you don't have one.

    Check which instance you're logged into. The app serves more than one program, and each one has its own instance.

    Instance Who
    krogerFred Meyer Kompass teams — this is you
    krogerdcThe Display Compliance scan team — different job, different team

    Make sure you're in kroger before you submit anything. Work submitted to the wrong instance isn't recorded against your reset, and you won't get the feedback that tells you what still needs addressing.

    Photograph after your lead has checked the set, not before. Shoot it first and you're photographing a section that's about to change.

    The photo is the record. A blurry or badly framed image doesn't just look sloppy — it can't be read, so the work it documents may as well not have been done.

    The capture order is the same order you set the shelf: start at Bay 1 and work left to right.

    One bay per photo. Every bay gets its own image, no matter how big or small the section is. Don't try to fit two bays in a frame and don't shoot a whole run at once.

    Four requirements on every shot:
    AngleStand directly in front of the middle of the bay and hold the camera square to the section, both vertically and horizontally. Shooting from an angle distorts product size from top to bottom and left to right, which is exactly the thing the photo is meant to show accurately
    Full sectionEvery product and every shelf edge visible. Top items complete — not clipped, bottom shelf in frame, and the shelf breaks at both sides captured
    ClaritySharp and in focus. No blur, no glare
    Glass doorsOpen the doors first, then shoot. Glass gives you reflection and distortion, and neither can be corrected afterwards

    Nothing in the way. No carts, no ladders, no shadow falling across the product, no teammate in frame. Move it and take the shot.

    Before you move to the next bay, ask yourself four questions:

    Check it on the spot. You're standing in front of the set right now, and you won't be in an hour.

    Submit in the app, then read what it gives you back.

    Store Intelligence doesn't just store the photo. It tells you what needs addressing — a facing that doesn't match the plan, a position that reads wrong, a gap it can see. That comes back to you while you're still in the aisle.

    Fix what it flags before you move on. That's the whole point of submitting from the floor rather than from the parking lot. Then re-shoot the bay so the record matches the corrected set.

    This is the loop:

    Set the bay → lead checks it → photograph it → submit → fix what comes back → re-shoot

    A bay that comes out clean is finished. Move to the next one.

    9.6 — What happens after you leave

    Your set doesn't stop being looked at when you walk out.

    Store Intelligence keeps working after the shift. If you submitted your own photos, you've already seen most of what it has to say and fixed it in the aisle — that's 9.5. Where the lead shoots instead, or where something surfaces later, the flag comes back to the team that worked the set rather than to the person standing in front of it.

    Either way, the fix is the same and it's cheaper the earlier it happens: two minutes in the aisle, a phone call the next day, or a return trip.

    Neither of these is a reason to be nervous. They're a reason to spend the extra two minutes in 9.1 while the cart is still beside you.

    9.7 — Clocking out

    Clock out in the Field App at the end of your shift, not in the aisle at the end of your last set. Section 3.4 covers the timing — you check the app before and after, not during.

    Confirm your hours the same day. A missed punch is easy to fix on the day it happens and increasingly annoying afterwards.

    9.8 — When the work isn't finished

    Sometimes a project doesn't close. A section wasn't ready, product didn't arrive, a planogram didn't match and the resolution didn't come back in time.

    Tell your lead before you leave, not after. What your lead needs to know:

    • What's done and what isn't
    • Exactly where you stopped — bay, shelf, position
    • What's blocking it
    • Where the materials and the pulled product are

    An unfinished set that's documented is a scheduling problem. An unfinished set nobody mentioned is a surprise for whoever opens that aisle tomorrow, and it costs three times as much to pick up.

    Leave it clean either way. Unfinished is not the same as abandoned — 9.2 and 9.3 still apply.

    Section 2 — What Is Kompass

    2.1 — The name

    KOMPASS stands for Kroger Optimization Management Plan Aligning Store Sales.

    It's Kroger's program for keeping shelf sets current — making sure what's on the shelf matches what Kroger has planned for it. New products go in, discontinued products come out, and the layout gets rebuilt to a new plan.

    You will hear this word constantly. Every reset you work, every note you're handed, every cart you pull from — Kompass. It is the name of the job you were hired to do.

    Scope: everything in this document describes Kompass work at Fred Meyer, a division of Kroger. Retail Odyssey serves other retailers, and their programs run differently. If you're ever assigned outside Fred Meyer, ask me before assuming any of this carries over.

    2.2 — How the week works

    Kompass typically runs on a weekly visit to each store. Some weeks the work calls for more — expect to be in the same store multiple days when the volume requires it.

    The work is split between two sides:

    Side Who What they own
    StoreThe Kompass Champion — usually the Operations Assistant Store ManagerPreps the store ahead of the visit, supports the team during it, signs off on completed work
    Vendor — usThe Retail Odyssey Kompass TeamExecutes resets, new item introductions, and maintenances against current planograms; walks the finished work with the store and provides sign-off sheets

    What that looks like in practice: a dedicated Kompass Champion is rarely staffed in stores anymore. Most of the prep, organization, and sign-off coordination has shifted onto our team and your lead. You should expect to absorb work the store standard says belongs to the store.

    That is not a complaint and it is not a reason to skip steps. The formal split still exists, and your lead uses it to set expectations during the walk-through. But if you arrive expecting someone to have staged everything for you, you'll be surprised. Plan to find things yourself and ask your lead when you can't.

    2.3 — What the store is supposed to have ready

    Knowing this list tells you what to look for when you arrive, and what to flag when it's missing:

    • Kompass Notes for the week, printed and filed
    • Kompass tags, filed by the store's Price Changer ahead of the visit
    • The Vestcom box, located and secured — this arrives by FedEx each week and holds your planograms, strips, and tags
    • The Kompass cart, organized and accessible, holding only the new items for this visit
    • Cleaning supplies, stocked and ready
    • A designated spot for perishable and frozen new items
    • Data Skins, enough on hand for upcoming resets

    Missing or wrong? Tell your lead. Planogram problems get escalated ahead of the visit where possible.

    2.4 — The two kinds of work

    Not every Kompass project is a full teardown. Know which one you've been assigned before you start pulling product.

    Full Reset — "Kompass Updates"

    The whole category is rebuilt to a new planogram.

    • Pull all discontinued items per the Kompass Notes — they carry closeout tags — and route them to the store associate or into clearly labeled boxes
    • Pull the existing strips from the set and install the new ones
    • Set the entire category to the new planogram
    • Rotate stock properly: older product to the front, newer product to the back of each facing
    • Pull all new-item stock from the back room onto the shelf
    • Wipe down all moved shelving and tag moldings
    • Hang every tag provided; hand-write tags where the store needs to recreate them
    • Move backstock to the designated associate or into labeled overstock boxes

    Partial — NIIs & Maintenances

    Only part of the set changes. The discipline here is restraint — you touch what changed and nothing else.

    • Pull discontinued items per the Kompass Notes, same as a full reset
    • Replace strips only on the shelves that changed — not the whole set
    • Set only the changing area of the planogram to the new strips
    • Where a category has no strips, cut the new items into the set per the planogram
    • Wipe down only the affected shelves — not the entire set
    • Hang every tag provided, or hand-write for the store to recreate
    • Move backstock to the designated associate or labeled overstock boxes

    Both types share one constant: keep the aisle shoppable the entire time. Materials, baskets, and carts stay on one side so customers always have a clear path through.

    2.5 — The shelf edge: data skins, strips, and tags

    Three separate things live on the front edge of the shelf, and new hires mix them up constantly. Learn the difference before your first reset.

    Part What it is
    Data skinThe clear plastic channel guide that snaps onto the front edge of the shelf. It's the hardware — it holds everything else
    StripThe printed label strip that slides into the data skin
    TagThe individual price tag for a single product

    The order is physical: skin snaps to the shelf, strip slides into the skin, tags sit on the strip.

    Why this matters on the job: a strip has nowhere to go if the data skin is missing, damaged, or the wrong size. On a full reset you're replacing every strip on the set, so damaged skins turn up as you work. On an NII or maintenance you're only touching the shelves that changed — but a bad skin on one of those shelves still stops you.

    Correcting data skins is part of NII and DI work. If you're short, tell your lead — the store is supposed to keep enough on hand for upcoming resets, and reorders get coordinated through the Kompass Team.

    2.6 — Rotation

    On every set you touch, older stock goes to the front and newer stock goes to the back of each facing. This is proper Key Retailing rotation, and it is not optional.

    The reason is simple: product that sits behind newer stock ages out and becomes a loss for the store. A perfectly set shelf with reversed rotation is a shelf that will generate out-of-code product in a few weeks.

    2.7 — Closing out the visit

    Your lead drives this, but you should know the sequence:

    1. Notify the Kompass Champion or Food Manager with an estimated completion time
    2. Complete the sign-off sheets — one for the store, plus a Summary Sign-Off for the Champion or Food Manager to sign
    3. Capture the signature — signing means the store agrees the work is complete to standard. Photograph the signed Summary page
    4. Walk every project with the store — show them where all overstock and discontinued items ended up
    5. Hand off — leave a copy of the completed sign-off sheets for the store's records

    Sign-off methods are in flux right now. Both fax and photo are in use — your lead will tell you which applies to your assignment.

    2.8 — Fred Meyer systems: what you sign, and what you don't

    CAO and Kompass Completion are Fred Meyer systems. They're the technology the store uses to sign out completed work at the end of a visit.

    Retail Odyssey employees do not have logins to either one, and are not permitted to sign work out in them. That's not a local rule or a matter of seniority — no one on our side has that access, including your lead and including me.

    Here's the clean division:

    Record Who owns it
    Sign-off sheets and the Summary Sign-Off pageUs — your lead prepares them, the store signs them, we keep a photo
    Entry in CAO / Kompass CompletionThe store, always

    So the sequence at the end of a visit is: we produce the paperwork, the store manager signs it, and the store enters it in their system on their own. If a store associate asks you to enter something for them, the answer is that you don't have access — point them to your lead.

    Our paperwork is our proof the work was done. Theirs is theirs.

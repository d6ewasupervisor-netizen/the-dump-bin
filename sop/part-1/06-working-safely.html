Section 6 — Working Safely

    Section 6 — Working Safely

    This is the longest section in the document, and it's the one worth reading twice. Reset work puts you on hard floors, up on step stools, around moving equipment, and with a blade in your hand — usually all in the same shift.

    Nothing here is complicated. Almost all of it is the kind of thing you already know and skip when you're moving fast.

    6.1 — If you see something unsafe, say so immediately

    Contact your lead the moment you notice a hazard — to you, to a teammate, to a store employee, or to a customer.

    Not at the end of the shift. Not after you've worked around it. Immediately.

    This includes things that aren't your problem and things you didn't cause: a damaged ceiling panel, a leaking case, a broken step stool, a fixture that moves when it shouldn't. You are not being difficult by reporting it. You're the person who happened to be standing there.

    6.2 — Working smart

    Most of this is attention rather than procedure.

    • Don't stand in front of swinging doors. Someone is coming through, and they can't see you
    • Slow down at blind corners — especially pushing a cart or carrying boxes
    • Use handrails on stairs. Turn the lights on in stairwells and any enclosed area before entering
    • Read warning and directional signs. They're posted because something happened
    • Never stack anything next to or in front of an electrical service panel. It has to stay clear
    • Use tools only as they're intended. A scraper is not a pry bar and a cutter is not a screwdriver

    6.3 — Working clean

    Keep your aisle clear. Carts, boxes, crates, and materials stay on one side so customers always have a path through. This is a safety rule before it's a service rule — a blocked aisle is where people trip and where carts collide.

    If something breaks, deal with it right then:

    1. Clean up the spill or damage immediately
    2. Mark the area until the floor is properly cleaned and no longer slippery
    3. Report it to your lead. Working alone, report it to the store manager or receiver

    Finding something to mark it with. We don't carry cones. Stores usually keep collapsible canvas cones tucked away at the ends of the aisles — behind an end cap, sometimes sitting on top of one. Look there first.

    If you can't find one, post something at the spot — or someone. Depending on the situation that might be a cart, a box, or a person standing there until it's handled. What it is matters less than that the hazard is visibly marked and nobody walks into it.

    Don't leave a wet floor unmarked, don't leave it unattended, and don't assume a store employee saw it.

    Handling trash:

    • Assume there's broken glass or something sharp in the bag, because eventually there will be
    • Hold trash away from your body
    • Never push down on a bag to compress it
    • Use a container to move trash whenever you can

    6.4 — Lifting, and moving shelving

    Before you lift anything:

    • Plan the move first. Look at the pathway and the destination. Contorting yourself around clutter halfway through a lift is how backs go
    • Test the load by lifting one side first
    • Uneven or unstable? Redistribute it, or get help
    • Too heavy? Split it into smaller loads, get a second person, or use a handling aid

    The lift itself:

    • Wide stance — feet at least shoulder width, one foot forward and to the side of the load
    • Back straight, knees bent, buttocks pushed out. Picture a weightlifter
    • Load as close to your body as you can get it. Distance from your body is what multiplies weight onto your spine
    • Head forward, so your spine stays aligned
    • Breathe out as you lift
    • Never twist. Turn with your feet — pivot, don't rotate your torso under load

    Twisting under load is the single most common way people hurt themselves doing this job, and it happens because turning your feet feels slower. It isn't. It's a half second.

    Two people lifting: talk to each other. Agree who lifts on what count, which way you're going, and where you're setting it down. Poor coordination is what turns a two-person lift into a one-person lift halfway across the floor.

    Above shoulder height: use a step stool or ladder. Never a box, a crate, or a shelf. See 6.6.

    Moving product off a shelf or rack: move it laterally to a nearby surface rather than lifting it down and away. Less distance under load, less reaching.

    • Never lift more than you're sure you can. Ask for help — nobody thinks less of you for it, and everybody thinks less of a back injury that was avoidable

    On shelving specifically:

    Never lean a shelf up against something and leave it unattended. Accidents happen fast, and there is no stable or safe position for a shelf other than locked into the gondola.

    Gondola is the term for the shelving unit that holds the shelves — the whole structure you're working on. You'll hear it constantly, so learn it now.

    If a shelf is out, it is either in your hands or locked back into the gondola. There is no third state.

    6.5 — Safety cutters

    You'll use one regularly, and familiarity is exactly what makes it dangerous.

    • Keep your free hand as far from the blade as possible
    • Hold the box firmly so it can't shift while you cut
    • Look at what you're cutting. Most cutter injuries happen to someone who was looking somewhere else
    • Replace dull blades often. A dull blade requires force, force slips, and slips cut people. A dull blade is more dangerous than a sharp one
    • Dispose of used blades properly — never in a trash bag someone else will lift
    • Carry and store it closed

    6.6 — Reaching high shelves

    A step stool or step ladder is the only approved way to reach a top shelf. Not a milk crate, not a shelf, not a cart, not anything else that happens to be nearby.

    Before you step on it

    Check the stool or ladder every time:

    Damaged? Don't use it. Remove it from the work area so nobody else picks it up, and report it to your lead or the store manager immediately.

    Setting it up

    • Dry, level surface. Never on top of something else
    • Completely open, with the spreaders locked
    • Check the weight capacity — it has to hold you and whatever you're carrying
    • Never in front of a door that isn't locked, blocked, or guarded
    • Never against a window

    On it

    • Keep your body centered. If you're leaning to reach, get down and move it
    • Always come down before repositioning it
    • Never move a ladder with a person or equipment on it
    • Never reach for a shelf higher than you can see. If you can't see the shelf, you can't see what's on it or what you're about to knock off it

    6.7 — Cold work

    Wear your safety gloves for every frozen reset, and any time the store requires them.

    Cold makes your hands clumsy and slow before it makes them cold enough to notice. Gloves are as much about grip and dexterity as they are about temperature.

    If it came out of a refrigerator or freezer, it goes back into one. That's a product rule, but it's in this section because the temptation to leave a case out "just for a minute" is what turns into a full pallet of loss.

    6.8 — If you get hurt

    Emergency: call 911. Don't hesitate, and don't wait to ask anyone first.

    For anything else, there's a process, and the first step of it is on a clock.
    1Within 24 hours of the injury, report the claim to the Workers' Compensation Intake Call Center — 888-900-4276, option 3, then option 1
    2See the in-network doctor you're referred to
    3Send your doctor's restrictions to the workers' comp department after the appointment — email or fax
    4Contact WC Claims to build a return-to-work or accommodation plan
    5Always tell your supervisor. Whatever else happens, and whoever else you've spoken to

    Go to every follow-up appointment. Missed appointments are the most common way a straightforward claim turns into a difficult one.

    The 24 hours is the part that matters most. A report inside 24 hours is routine. A report after it is a claim someone has to justify, and you're the one being asked to justify it — weeks later, about an injury you're still recovering from.

    6.9 — Equipment

    Equipment you may use:

    Hand trucks, dollies, U-boats, and manual pallet jacks — following all posted safety guidelines.

    • They are always walked, whether loaded or empty
    • Never ride one, and never let anyone else ride one

    Equipment you do not operate, ever:

    • Electric pallet jacks
    • Forklifts

    Only store employees are authorized to operate powered equipment. If someone asks you to move something with a powered jack, the answer is that you're not authorized. Find your lead.

    Balers: two conditions, both required.

    • You must be 18 or older to operate a baler
    • You must be shown how to use that specific baler before you use it

    Posted directions on the machine are not training. If nobody has walked you through that baler, don't operate it — ask your lead. Once you have been shown, follow the posted safety guidelines exactly.
